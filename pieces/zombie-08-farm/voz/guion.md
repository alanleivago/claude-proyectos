## 1. THE ALERT (0:00) — sin tarjeta (abre el teléfono, 6:42 AM)

At six forty-two in the morning, your phone goes off. Zombie outbreak, day one. Shelter in place, and avoid populated areas. And you're on a farm, forty miles from the nearest town… which, honestly, is one of the best places you could be right now. You've got a well, a barn full of feed, twelve cows, a loud bunch of chickens and one very good dog… and before midnight, that dog is going to save your life. But only if you skip the mistake almost everybody makes on day one. So you step out onto the porch with your coffee, and you take a look at what you've got. There's the house, the big red barn, the well, the old silo, and two hundred acres of corn that nobody is harvesting this year. The closest neighbor is two miles away, and the only way in is one gravel road with a gate at the end. The house is old, but it has a woodstove, a root cellar, and a basement full of your grandma's canned peaches. You've got water, food, fuel, tools and land, which is more than most people in town will have by tonight. And when you stand still and listen… all you can hear is the chickens.

Right now every road around you is filling up with cars, and they're all heading the same way… toward town. So you take the truck anyway, and twenty minutes later you're stuck in a traffic jam that hasn't moved in ten minutes… with your window down.

Yeah, let's not do that. Even if town was fine this morning, by noon every store will be empty, and you'd be stuck right in the middle of it. Farm rule number one, you stay away from the places where people pile up, like towns, highways, gas stations and shelters. That's where the crowds end up today, and every crowd is a meal waiting to happen. Out here you're two miles from anyone, and that distance is the best thing you own, so you keep it. The truck stays in the yard.

## 3. THE WATER CLOCK — tarjeta RE: 7:20 AM / THE WATER CLOCK

The next thing you think about is water, and on a farm it's trickier than it sounds. Your water comes from a well, and that well has an electric pump sitting down at the bottom. So it works like this. The grid powers the house, the house powers the pump, and the pump pushes the water up to your faucet. If the power goes out… the pump stops, and so does your water. And with nobody showing up to work at the power plant, the grid isn't going to last long out here. Maybe an hour, maybe less, so you start the clock and you fill everything you can. The bathtub goes first, and that's about forty gallons right there. Then you fill ten one-gallon jugs and four five-gallon buckets, so that's thirty more. And the water heater in the basement is already full, which is another forty. You remember that one from the police station. That's a hundred and ten gallons. Ready dot gov says one gallon per person per day, for drinking and washing, so for you alone that's more than three months. But then you remember the cows… and a cow drinks somewhere around ten to twenty gallons a day. You have twelve of them. So your whole stash would keep them going for about half a day. The cows get the pond behind the barn, and the bathtub stays yours. You open the gate to the back field, so they can walk down to the water on their own. At seven fifty-one the kitchen lights flicker… and then the fridge goes quiet.

## 4. THE GENERATOR — tarjeta RE: 8:10 AM / THE GENERATOR

Good news, there's a diesel generator in the shed. You hook it up to the well, and you pull the cord… and it's a lot louder than you remember. A regular generator puts out about seventy decibels at twenty-three feet, that's the distance they get tested at. Every time you double the distance, it only drops about six decibels… so six hundred and fifty feet out it's still around forty, about as loud as a library. On a farm with no traffic and no wind, that's plenty to hear… and something out in the corn heard the noise. So you shut it off, and you give it some rules. It runs outside, never in the house, the garage or the barn, and never up on the porch. It sits at least twenty feet from any door or window, with the exhaust pointing away from the house, because that exhaust is full of carbon monoxide… and you can't see it or smell it before it puts you on the floor. And it only runs when you need it, so twenty minutes around noon to fill the tub and the troughs, and never after dark. On a cold, still night, sound carries even farther than it does at noon. Twenty minutes of noise a day, and not one minute more.

## 5. NOISY ANIMALS — tarjeta RE: 11:30 AM / NOISY ANIMALS

The generator isn't the only loud thing on this farm, though. Twelve cows moo whenever they feel like it, twenty-four chickens never stop talking, and the rooster starts at five in the morning. You can't make a farm quiet, so you make the noise work for you. You bring everybody in close. The cows go in the pen next to the house, and the chickens go in the coop right by the porch. If something walks up in the dark, they'll freak out long before you'd ever see it. Cows bunch up and bellow when something is wrong, and a scared chicken goes off like a car alarm. It also means feeding time is a thirty-step walk instead of half a mile out in the open. And then there's Ribbon. A dog can hear some sounds about four times farther away than you can… so her bed goes right by the front door, and she's your alarm now. She takes the job very seriously, mostly against the chickens. By evening the whole farm is tucked in around the house, and it's the loudest little neighborhood in the county.

## 6. LOCK THE GATE — tarjeta RE: 3:15 PM / LOCK THE GATE

In the middle of the afternoon, you walk down to the front gate. It's the only way a car can get onto the farm, and right now it's wide open. So you park the truck sideways right behind it, nose to the fence, and nobody is ramming through that. You wrap the chain twice around the post, and you lock it with the big padlock from the shed. Click. A chained gate stops any car cold, and it slows down anything walking on two legs. Then you do the house, and you already know how to fix a door… three-inch screws through the strike plate, straight into the stud, same as last time. It takes ten minutes and a drill, and it's the difference between a door that holds and a door that flies open. The back door gets the same treatment, and after dark every curtain on the ground floor stays shut, with the lights off. Out here, one lit window shows up from a long way off, and you don't want to be the only light for miles. By six o'clock the whole farm is locked up tight.

## 7. INVENTORY — tarjeta RE: 6:40 PM / INVENTORY

Before the sun goes down, you sit at the kitchen table and you write everything down. It's the same list you'd make in any survival game, except this time there's no reloading if you get it wrong. Water, a hundred and ten gallons. Food, about thirty-four days, as long as you like canned beans. The freezer is full of meat, and a full freezer only stays cold for about two days with the door shut, so that gets cooked first. Diesel, twenty-two gallons, so that's a lot of twenty-minute refills. Animal feed, twenty-six days. That's hay and corn for the cows, plus four sacks of layer feed for the chickens. And medicine… zero. There's no First Aid Spray this time, so whatever happens out here, you really can't get hurt. On paper, you could last for weeks. You lock the door, you check it twice, and for the first time today you sit down. Day one, you save your game.
