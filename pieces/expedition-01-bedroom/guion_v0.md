# Mechayuki Expeditions 01: The Bedroom (horizontal 16:9, ~11 min)

**Title options**
1. **How Did Humans Sleep Before Beds? An Android Excavates Your Bedroom** (recommended: a question people search for, plus the android twist)
2. 3,000 Years From Now, an Android Finds Your Bedroom
3. What Did Humans Actually Sleep On? An Android Digs Up a Bedroom

**Structure** (`fuentes/mechayuki-largo/analisis-viralidad.md`): one ruin, one theme (sleep), 8 linked objects. The video opens and closes in the present (the buried bedroom as an MML2-style dungeon). Each object opens a trip to the past, with an ITEM GET menu as the chapter card. There are 3 "Human Body Manual" segments, and the closing has a "boss": a mystery object for the comments.

**Length:** 1,434 words of narration. At the megagirl pace (0.436 s/word) that is ~10:25 of voice, plus ~50 s of ITEM GET menus and stings, so about **11:15**. If the voice comes out slower, the first cut is the dolphin line in Body Manual 3.

**Voice:** megagirl with the clean chain, no "!". Written conversationally (humanizer rules: connectors, no telegraphic lists). Questions that must rise: the hook question, "what was it for?" and the closing question.

## 0:00 — Hook (present, the buried bedroom)

Operator… my detector says there's something under this hill. Something big… and soft.

Humans spent one third of their whole lives in this room. Lying down, eyes closed, completely unconscious… on purpose. Every single night.

It's called a bedroom. And today I'm going to find out how humans slept… before any of this existed.

## 0:25 — Entering the dungeon

The door is stuck, so I'll use the window. The ceiling held up, but there's two meters of dirt on the carpet. Scanning…

Posters on the walls, clothes on the floor, a cup with something green growing inside… This human was not tidy.

Eight human artifacts in one small room. All of them for the same activity: doing nothing for eight hours. Let's dig.

## 0:54 — ITEM 1: The Mattress (present → past)

First artifact. A rectangle full of metal springs, wrapped in foam and fabric. Humans lay on top of it and stayed there all night. My scan says it's one of the most expensive objects in the house… and it's for sleeping.

So what did humans sleep on before springs? Let's go back. Way back.

Two hundred thousand years ago, in a cave in South Africa, humans pulled up grass and laid it on the floor to sleep on. And here's the clever part: under the grass, they spread ash from the fire. Scientists think it kept crawling insects away from their bodies. The first mattress came with built-in bug spray.

Then for thousands of years, not much changed. In medieval Europe, most people slept on sacks stuffed with straw. And the straw had guests. Fleas, lice, bedbugs… everyone just accepted them as part of the bed. Rich people changed the straw more often. Most people changed it… about once a year.

Humans invented springs. The bugs still haven't left.

## 2:12 — ITEM 2: The Pillow

Second artifact. A soft bag for the head. Humans hugged it, folded it, flipped it over to find the cold side…

The first pillows we know of come from Mesopotamia, around nine thousand years ago. They were made of stone. In ancient Egypt, rich people rested their heads on carved wooden headrests, and they took them into their tombs. In China, people slept on pillows made of porcelain. Hard, cool, and beautifully painted.

Humans used to sleep on rocks… and they liked it.

And no, people in the Middle Ages didn't sleep sitting up. The paintings show them propped up on pillows, but that's mostly just how artists drew beds. Some doctors told sick people to keep their heads up. Everyone else slept lying down, like you.

## 3:11 — ITEM 3: The Blanket (sharing a bed)

Third artifact. A heavy blanket, big enough for two humans. My scan found hairs from four different people on it. Including a dog.

For most of history, sleeping alone was rare. Whole families shared one bed to stay warm. When humans traveled, the inn could put you in a bed… with a total stranger. And there were rules: lie still, stay quiet, and don't steal the blankets.

In 1776, two American founding fathers, John Adams and Benjamin Franklin, had to share a tiny bed at a crowded inn. Adams closed the window because he was afraid of the cold night air. Franklin opened it again, and lectured him about his theory of colds… until Adams fell asleep.

A future president and a scientist, fighting over a window. I would have closed it too.

## 4:13 — HUMAN BODY MANUAL 1: Why do you sleep?

Human Body Manual. Page one. Why do humans shut down every night?

While you're awake, your brain works nonstop, and it makes waste. When you fall asleep, a fluid washes through your brain and carries that waste away. In a famous study with mice, the spaces between brain cells opened up by about sixty percent during sleep, so the cleaning could flow faster.

So when you sleep… your brain is literally taking out the trash. Adults need about seven hours a night to do it properly.

I recharge in forty minutes. Just saying.

## 4:58 — ITEM 4: The Lamp (two sleeps)

Fourth artifact. A lamp. You press a button, and the night is gone.

Before electricity, light was expensive. Poor families burned candles made of animal fat that smoked and smelled terrible. Or a rushlight: a dried plant dipped in grease that burned for about twenty minutes.

So when the sun went down, the night was really, really dark. And historian Roger Ekirch found hundreds of old diaries and letters that describe something strange. People went to bed a few hours after dark… then woke up around midnight for an hour or so. They prayed, talked, visited the neighbors, and then went back to bed. They called it "first sleep" and "second sleep."

Then cheap artificial light arrived, people started staying up later… and the two sleeps slowly turned into one.

## 5:59 — HUMAN BODY MANUAL 2: Dreams

Human Body Manual. Page two. What happens inside the shut-down human?

Sleep comes in cycles of about ninety minutes. And in 1953, two scientists in Chicago noticed something weird: every so often, sleeping people's eyes start moving fast under their eyelids. They called it R-E-M, rapid eye movement. Wake someone up during it, and they'll usually tell you a dream.

So every night, humans lie completely still… while their eyes watch a movie that doesn't exist.

## 6:37 — Back in the dungeon

Back to the present. The wardrobe collapsed, so I'm digging around it. One more drawer… and the drawer is full of the same soft clothes. Twenty-seven pieces, and all of them with little moons on them. This human had a favorite.

## 6:57 — ITEM 5: Pajamas

Fifth artifact. Soft clothes with little moons printed on them. Humans wear them only to sleep.

The word "pajamas" comes from Persian: "pāy," leg, and "jāma," clothing. Leg clothes. The British picked them up in India in the 1800s and took them home as sleepwear. Before that, most Europeans slept in a long nightshirt.

## 7:25 — ITEM 6: The Nightstand

Sixth artifact. A small table next to the bed. Today it holds a phone, a glass of water and a book nobody finishes.

But before indoor toilets, its main job was hiding the chamber pot. A little cabinet with a door, so you didn't have to see it. In fancy French houses, that kind of cabinet was called a "commode." And that's why today, a toilet can be called… a commode.

Your nightstand used to be a toilet closet. I'm not touching it.

## 8:06 — ITEM 7: Earplugs

Seventh artifact. Two small foam cylinders. Humans pushed them into their ears to block out other humans.

The oldest earplugs in any story are made of beeswax. In Homer's Odyssey, Odysseus plugs his sailors' ears so they won't hear the deadly song of the Sirens. Then in 1907, a German pharmacist named Max Negwer read that story and started selling wax earplugs. His company still makes them today.

Humans needed a myth… to invent a quiet night. And my scan says this pair was used a lot. Somebody in this house snored.

## 8:50 — ITEM 8: The Alarm (phone)

Eighth artifact. A phone, still plugged in next to the pillow. Its last setting: an alarm at six-thirty… snoozed eight times.

The same object was the alarm, the clock, the lamp, the radio and the bedtime story. Humans took the whole world into bed with them… and then wondered why they couldn't sleep.

Operators who watched my last expedition already know this one. Before alarm clocks, workers paid a knocker-upper to tap on their windows, or shoot dried peas at them. And in 1956, humans invented the snooze button.

## 9:33 — HUMAN BODY MANUAL 3: No sleep

Human Body Manual. Page three. What happens if a human doesn't sleep at all?

In 1964, a seventeen-year-old named Randy Gardner stayed awake for eleven days straight, for a school science project. By day three he was moody and clumsy. By day five, he was seeing things that weren't there. He slept fourteen hours afterwards and recovered. Guinness later stopped tracking the record, because it's too dangerous.

Dolphins do it smarter. They sleep with half of their brain at a time… and keep one eye open.

## 10:16 — Closing (present) + the boss

Let me check my inventory. Eight artifacts. Grass beds, stone pillows, strangers sharing blankets, two sleeps a night, pajamas from Persia, a toilet cabinet, wax from the Odyssey, and peas against the window.

So here's my conclusion about humans. For two hundred thousand years, you worked on the perfect bed. Grass, straw, springs, foam… And then you put a glowing rectangle next to your pillow that keeps you awake.

But there's one more artifact. It was hidden under the bed. A glass bottle full of liquid, with a blob of wax inside that slowly floats up… and slowly sinks down. Over and over. It doesn't light the room. It doesn't tell the time. It doesn't make any sound.

Operators… what was it for? Tell me in the comments.

Expedition complete. Logging off.
