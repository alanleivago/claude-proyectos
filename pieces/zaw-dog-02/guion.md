# How You're Accidentally Ruining Your Dog's Trust (zaw-dog #2)

EN, about 39 s, 117 words, deadpan narrator. Cast: the approved brown-and-white mixed-breed dog, the photo-built owner (white tee, jeans), a researcher in a lab coat, a second pointer, and two strangers. Every extra person gets their own face and body, never an owner recolour. Cosy home sets: living room, kitchen, hallway. The last line runs straight into the first, so the video loops mid-sentence.

This is the dog version of ZawPolyShorts' "How You're Accidentally Ruining Your Cat's Trust" (12M). Their cat version is advice with no source: don't drag a scared cat out from under the bed. Our twist is a habit every dog owner has, the fake throw, plus two real Kyoto University experiments. In one, dogs stopped following a person who had pointed them to an empty cup. In the other, dogs avoided a stranger who refused to help their owner.

## Fact check (2026-11-06)
- **Pointing study (lines 2–8):** Takaoka, Maeda, Hori & Fujita (2015), "Do dogs follow behavioral cues from an unreliable human?", *Animal Cognition* 18:475–483, Kyoto University, 34 pet dogs. A treat was hidden in one of two containers. In Phase 1, "most dogs followed pointing". In Phase 2, the dogs were shown what was in both containers, and then the experimenter pointed at the empty one. In Phase 3, the procedure repeated Phase 1, and "many fewer" dogs followed. In Experiment 2, the dogs did follow a new experimenter, which rules out tiredness or boredom. The script says "most" and "far fewer" to match the abstract. It never says "none", because popular coverage overstates the result.
- **Fake throw (lines 1, 13):** this is our extrapolation. The study used pointing, not throwing, and the dogs had seen the empty cup beforehand. That's why line 1 says "might" and line 2 says "something close". The study tested the effect immediately, so how long the distrust lasts is unknown, and the script doesn't claim it lasts.
- **Helper study (lines 10–12):** Chijiiwa, Kuroshima, Hori, Anderson & Fujita (2015), "Dogs avoid people who behave negatively to their owner: third-party affective evaluation", *Animal Behaviour* 106:123–127, Kyoto University, 54 dogs (18 per condition). The owner tried to open a clear container holding a roll of tape (worthless to a dog), and the "actor" turned away when asked for help. Then the actor and a neutral person each offered the same treat, without looking at the dog. In the Nonhelper condition, the dogs took food from the neutral person "more often than the nonhelpful actor". In the Helper and Control conditions, their choices were random. The script says "more often", because the press release gives no percentages. The dogs showed no preference for helpers, so the script doesn't claim they like helpful people. "Keep tabs on" in line 9 is a loose deadpan paraphrase of "avoid people who are unhelpful to their owner".
- **Left out on purpose:** the "dogs know when you lie" headline, because the study is about reliability, not lying. Also left out: a 2021 follow-up where cats did not show this avoidance. It's true, but there was no room, and it's a good hook for a later video.
- **Script changes after the check:** "most dogs stopped going" became "far fewer dogs bothered", and "the dogs refused his snack" became "took the other one's snack more often".
