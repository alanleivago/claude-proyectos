# Your Dog Thinks You're Hurt When You Cry (zaw-dog #3)

EN, about 37 s, 112 words, deadpan narrator, one cut per sentence (14 cuts). The cast is the approved v2 dog (pieces/zaw-dog-v2), the photo-built owner in a white tee and jeans, and a woman stranger with her own face, body and clothes (never an owner recolour). The last line ends mid-sentence and runs into line 1, so the video loops. The hero bark sits in the comma gap of line 1, between "crying," and "and", and never covers a word.

This is the dog version of ZawPolyShorts' "Your Cat Thinks You're Weak When You Shower" (18.6M), and it follows the same shape. The owner does something ordinary, and the animal doesn't get why. The animal reads it as danger and comes to the door to help. The cat video has no source. Ours uses two real experiments: a fake-crying test in owners' living rooms, and a door test where owners cried or hummed behind a see-through door.

## Fact check (2026-11-08)
- **Custance & Mayer 2012 (lines 3-9):** "Empathic-like responding by domestic dogs (*Canis familiaris*) to distress in humans: an exploratory study", *Animal Cognition* 15(5):851-859, Goldsmiths, University of London. There were 18 pet dogs, tested in their owners' homes. The owner and a stranger (co-author Jennifer Mayer) took turns talking, humming and pretending to cry. 15 of the 18 dogs approached during crying, against 6 during humming. The dogs approached whoever was crying, and when the stranger cried they sniffed, nuzzled and licked her instead of going to their owner. Of the 15 that approached a crier, 13 did so with submissive body language (tail tucked, head bowed), which is why line 9 says "most". The press gives no figure for talking, so the script never gives one.
- **"Thinks you're hurt" (lines 1, 14):** this is the deadpan title frame, borrowed from Zaw's "thinks you're weak". The authors say the behaviour looks like empathic concern but is best explained by emotional contagion plus a learned history of being rewarded for approaching upset people. The script never claims the dog understands sadness.
- **Sanford, Burt & Meyers-Manor 2018 (lines 10-13):** "Timmy's in the well: Empathy and prosocial helping in dogs", *Learning & Behavior* (2018, DOI 10.3758/s13420-018-0332-3). There were 34 dogs. The owner sat behind a clear door held shut with magnets and either cried or hummed "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star". Crying did **not** make more dogs open the door: a similar share opened it in both conditions (line 12). The dogs that did open it were about three times faster when the owner cried (line 13). The script never says every dog comes to the rescue. The dogs that opened the door also showed less stress; it's true, but there was no room for it.
- **Left out on purpose:** the "help" calls some retellings add to the door test (not confirmed in the sources checked), the stress result, and the exact latencies in seconds (not in the press coverage checked). Also left out: Macalester College as the place of the experiment. JHU coverage ties it to Sanford's undergraduate work, but the on-screen text only says "THE DOOR TEST, 2018" to stay safe.
- **Script changes after the check:** none needed. "Fifteen dogs" and "Humming got six" match the published numbers, and "Most came in heads low" matches 13 of 15.
