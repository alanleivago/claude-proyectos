In this world, everyone is holding the same rope.
It runs through every city, across every ocean, and into the Fog at the edge of the world.
Something on the other side pulls back. So nobody lets go.
You get your own knot the day you're born. Babies get tiny gloves.
Weddings are one-handed. The ring goes on the hand you're not pulling with.
Mail slides along the rope on little hooks. A birthday card takes nine years to reach the other end.
And sneezing is illegal. One guy sneezed in 1971. The world lost an inch. Nobody's forgiven him.
My family holds the first knot. Right where the Fog starts.
Grandma Oona has held it for sixty years. Every night she hums into the Fog.
Sometimes the Fog hums back.
She always says, "Every rope has two ends, and both of them are tired."
At recess we play tug of war. I lose on purpose. I always feel bad for the other team.
Once a year, on Heave Day, the whole world pulls at once and we win one step.
There's one rule they teach you in kindergarten: the rope only moves when both sides move.
Nobody knows what it means.
Then one night, the rope started to slip.
One meter. Then ten. Every night the Fog came closer, and every night it dragged Grandma closer to the edge.
So the world pulled harder.
Tractors. Elephants. Eight billion people, all at once.
The harder we pulled, the harder they pulled.
On the seventh night, Grandma's feet were in the Fog, and she stopped humming.
So I went in.
Hand over hand along the rope. I couldn't see my own fingers.
Then I heard it. Grandma's song, coming from the other side.
The rope went up a mountain and over a ridge of rock. I climbed to the top and looked down.
There was another world down there. Another city. Another eight billion people, holding the same rope.
And on their first knot, a girl my age. Humming Grandma's song.
Behind her, the whole mountain was sliding toward the sea. Houses, roads, everything.
They weren't pulling against us. They were hanging on.
Every time we pulled, they slipped. Every time they slipped, the rope dragged us into the Fog.
We weren't playing tug of war. We were holding each other up.
I always feel bad for the other team. This time, the other team was real.
So I yelled down to her. "On three, we both step up!"
One. Two. Three.
We stepped. The rope didn't slip. It moved.
The rope only moves when both sides move.
Step by step, sixteen billion people climbed, until the Fog thinned out and we could see each other.
Grandma and the girl's grandma waved. First time in sixty years.
Now Heave Day is Wave Day. Once a year, both sides take one step up and wave from the ridge.
Nobody lets go.
Now we know who we're holding.
