# Digger 03: The lock (~220 words, ~85-95 s)

Operator… I found another human artifact. Humans called it… a lock.

If you roll a rock against your door… you have a lock. But now you can't get out either.

So humans put a wooden bar across the door. This is a bolt.

But from the outside… anyone could slide it open.

Four thousand years ago, wooden pins dropped into the bolt and locked it. A wooden key with pegs lifted them out. This is a pin lock.

Operator Mia asks: "Why do keys have little teeth?" Mia… keep watching.

The Romans made locks out of metal… with keys so small they wore them as rings.

But someone always got in anyway. A thief.

Sneaky.

He slid thin wires into the lock… and lifted the pins one by one. This is lock picking.

In 1851, a locksmith named Alfred Hobbs picked the famous "unpickable" lock… in fifty-one hours.

In the 1860s, Linus Yale Junior put the pins inside a small turning cylinder… with a flat key.

Only the right key lifts every pin to the same line… and the cylinder turns. That's why keys have little teeth. This is a pin tumbler.

Your front door still works like that lock from four thousand years ago.

Humans spent four thousand years… just to keep other humans out?

Artifact understood. Logging off.

## Fact check (web, 2026-10-27)
| line | verdict | sources |
|---|---|---|
| A rock or a bar across the door as the first "lock" | illustrative setup, not a dated claim. The bar (bolt) across the door from the inside is the classic pre-lock closure | [Britannica: lock](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lock-security/Introduction) |
| Four thousand years ago: wooden pins drop into holes in the bolt; a wooden key with pegs lifts them | correct. The oldest known lock (Khorsabad, near Nineveh) is possibly 4,000 years old and is the wooden pin type called the "Egyptian lock" because it was so widely used in Egypt. Britannica describes a wooden bolt with holes, pins that drop into them, and a toothbrush-shaped key with pegs that lifts the pins. The script says "humans", not "Egyptians", to avoid the origin dispute | [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lock-security/Introduction), [Illinois Extension: Introduction to Keys and Locks](https://web.extension.illinois.edu/artifact/shared/pdf/intro/Introduction%20to%20Keys%20and%20Locks.pdf) |
| The Romans made metal locks, with keys small enough to wear as rings | correct. Romans made bronze and iron locks and keys; Roman key rings (a ring with a small key bit) are common finds in London, Colchester and Pompeii | [V&A key ring O377794](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O377794/ring/), [V&A ring O122113](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O122113/ring-unknown), [Lock and key (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lock_and_key) |
| A thief lifts the pins one by one with thin wires (lock picking) | correct as the general principle of picking a tumbler lock | [Pin tumbler lock (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) |
| 1851: locksmith Alfred Hobbs picked a lock famous for being unpickable, in 51 hours | correct. A. C. Hobbs, an American locksmith, picked the Bramah "challenge" lock in 1851, during the Great Exhibition, after it had resisted for decades. It took 51 hours over 16 days. One source says 44 hours; Wikipedia gives 51 | [Bramah lock (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bramah_lock), [Alfred Charles Hobbs (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Charles_Hobbs), [Mental Floss](https://www.mentalfloss.com/history/man-who-picked-victorian-londons-unpickable-lock) |
| In the 1860s, Linus Yale Jr. put the pins in a small turning cylinder with a flat key with teeth | correct. Yale Jr. patented the cylinder pin-tumbler lock in 1861 and in 1865 (US 48,475), with a small flat serrated key. His father, Linus Yale Sr., had patented a pin-tumbler lock in 1848. The script says "the 1860s" to cover both patents | [Lemelson-MIT: Linus Yale](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/linus-yale), [Lockwiki: Linus Yale Jr](https://lockwiki.com/index.php/Linus_Yale_Jr), [ASME](https://www.asme.org/topics-resources/content/linus-yale-jr), [Pin tumbler lock (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) |
| The right key lifts every pin to the same line (the shear line) and the cylinder turns; that's why keys have teeth | correct (the key cuts lift each key and driver pin pair so the split lines up with the shear line) | [Pin tumbler lock (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pin_tumbler_lock) |
| Your front door uses the same idea as a lock from 4,000 years ago | correct as a simplification. Britannica and the Illinois guide both say the Yale pin tumbler uses the same principle as the ancient Egyptian lock | [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/technology/lock-security/Introduction), [Illinois Extension](https://web.extension.illinois.edu/artifact/shared/pdf/intro/Introduction%20to%20Keys%20and%20Locks.pdf), [Greatpatent](https://greatpatent.com/the-yale-lock-copies-a-four-thousand-year-old-egyptian-design) |
