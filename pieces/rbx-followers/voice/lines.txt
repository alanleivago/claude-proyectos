In this town, everyone has followers. And they really do follow you.
Every follower is a real person, walking right behind you, wherever you go.
There's only one way to lose one. Turn around and look them in the eye, and they stop following you. Forever.
That's why famous people never look back.
A pop star with ninety million followers took four years to cross Canada.
First dates are crowded. You bring everyone.
And when a cat goes viral, the highway closes for a month.
I've had one follower since the day I was born. Just one.
I've never seen who it is. I never look back.
But I know the sound. Squeak. Step. Squeak. One of their shoes always squeaks.
My dad works nights. He's never home when I'm awake.
He leaves me notes on the fridge, and every note ends the same way.
"Don't look back, kiddo. Whatever's behind you, I've got it."
Then one day, I slipped and fell into the fountain at the mall.
Somebody filmed it. By lunch, it had three million views.
The next morning, I opened the front door, and the whole street was full.
Three million and one people. All waiting for me.
I walked to school. They walked to school.
They followed me into math class. The teacher gave up and taught all of them.
If I stopped, they pushed. If I turned left, the whole city turned left.
I couldn't even go home. There was no room for my house in the crowd.
So I tried to lose them.
I jumped on the subway. Three million people got on behind me.
I grabbed a megaphone and yelled, "Unfollow!" Nobody left.
Three days. No sleep. No home.
And somewhere in all that noise, I couldn't hear the squeak anymore.
There was only one way out. I had to turn around.
But if I turned around, I'd lose all of them. Even the one who'd been there since day one.
I stopped in the middle of the bridge, with three million strangers breathing down my neck.
My legs were shaking. They were chanting my name. Dad's last note was still in my pocket.
And then, really quiet, right behind me, I heard it.
Squeak. Step. Squeak.
So I turned around.
Three million people blinked, shrugged, and walked away.
The number above my head said zero.
And there, three steps back, was a man in a night-shift vest. One old shoe. Squeaking.
My dad.
He never worked nights. He told me that so I'd never look for him.
For eleven years, he'd walked three steps behind me. Every crosswalk. Every bad day.
He wasn't following me. He was walking me home.
He said, "You looked."
I said, "I wanted to see who it was."
Now we walk side by side.
He still squeaks. I still never look back.
I don't have to.
Zero followers.
Never alone.
