## 0 · Cold open

You've heard of the Trojan Horse. You've never heard what it cost the man who built it: ten years, every ship, every friend.

On the way home he met a giant with one eye, a witch who turned his men into pigs, and a monster with six heads on six long necks. He went down among the dead and watched them crowd around a pit of blood. And when he finally got home, he walked into his own house dressed as a beggar, with a bow, and a hundred men inside who wanted his wife.

He left Troy with twelve ships and about six hundred men, and he came home alone. The sea took some of them, and the monsters took others. But if you follow the story closely, you keep finding Odysseus himself at the start of every disaster.

So let's start at the beginning, with the horse.

## 1 · The Horse

For ten years the Greeks threw themselves at the walls of Troy, and for ten years the walls held.

Achilles was dead by then, along with most of the men worth anything. The kings were tired of each other, and the ships were rotting on the beach.

Odysseus was the king of Ithaca, a small rocky island nobody had much reason to visit. He wasn't the strongest of the Greeks, or the bravest. What he had was a mind that never stopped looking for the trick.

His trick was a horse. A huge wooden one, hollow inside, left in front of the gates as an offering to the gods. Then the whole Greek army burned its camp and sailed away.

The Trojans came out and found the beach empty. After ten years, the enemy had simply gone. So they dragged the horse into the city, and they drank and danced around it, and eventually they went to sleep.

That night a hatch opened in its belly. Odysseus climbed down a rope with his best men and opened the gates from the inside, and the army, which had only been hiding behind an island, came back in the dark.

By morning Troy was ash.

*[BEAT: open loop — the gods were watching]*

The Greeks had won, but they hadn't won cleanly. They burned temples, and they dragged people away from the altars where they'd gone to beg the gods for help. The gods saw all of it.

Odysseus loaded his twelve ships with gold and slaves and wine and turned toward home. Ithaca was a few weeks' sail away.

It took him ten years.

## 2 · Ismarus and the Lotus-Eaters

The first place they stopped was a city called Ismarus, which had nothing to do with Troy at all.

Odysseus attacked it anyway. His men killed the men, took the women, and carried off the wine and the cattle. It was easy and it was profitable, and when it was done Odysseus told them to get back to the ships.

They didn't listen. They were drunk on the wine and on how easy it had been, so they stayed on the beach roasting cattle, and while they ate, the neighbours of the city came down out of the hills.

By the time the ships pushed off, seventy-two of his men were lying dead on the sand. Six from every ship.

*[BEAT: first count of the dead — keep a running tally on screen]*

Then a storm caught them. For nine days the wind dragged them south, off the edge of every map they knew, and on the tenth day they came to a gentle country where people lived on the fruit of a flower called the lotus. Odysseus sent three men ashore to say hello.

They didn't come back.

When he went after them, he found them sitting with the Lotus-Eaters, smiling, eating the flower. Nobody had hurt them and nobody was holding them there. They just didn't want to go home anymore. I think that's the strangest danger in the whole story, because you can't fight it. They had forgotten home existed.

Odysseus dragged them back to the ships in tears, tied them under the rowing benches, and made everyone else row before anybody else could taste the stuff.

## 3 · The Cyclops

Next they found an island full of wild goats, and across a narrow stretch of water, the land of the Cyclopes. These were giant shepherds with a single eye in the middle of the forehead. They had no laws and no ships, and they had no fear of the gods at all.

The sensible thing was to leave them alone. Odysseus wanted to see them, though. He wanted to know who they were and whether they'd give him gifts, the way a host was supposed to.

So he took twelve men and a skin of very strong wine and climbed to a cave above the sea.

The cave was full: cheeses stacked on racks, pens crowded with lambs and kids, buckets of milk. The owner was out with his flock. His men begged him to grab the cheese and a few lambs and run.

Odysseus said no. He wanted to meet him.

At evening the owner came home. His name was Polyphemus, and he was a son of Poseidon, god of the sea. He drove his flock inside, picked up a stone that twenty-two wagons couldn't have shifted, and closed the door of the cave with it.

Then he lit a fire, and he saw them.

Odysseus stepped forward and spoke the way a guest is supposed to. We're Greeks, on our way home from Troy, he said. The gods protect travellers, and we ask for your hospitality.

The Cyclops laughed at him. We don't care about your gods, he said. We're stronger than they are.

Then he grabbed two of the men, smashed them against the floor like puppies, and ate them, bones and all. He washed them down with milk, lay down among his sheep, and went to sleep.

Odysseus drew his sword. He could have killed him right there. But then they'd all die in that cave anyway, because no man alive could move the stone in the doorway.

So they waited.

In the morning the Cyclops ate two more men for breakfast, took his flock out, and rolled the stone back into place behind him.

*[BEAT: the plan, shown as a black-figure sequence — stake, fire, wine]*

Lying in the cave was a club of green olive wood, as long as a ship's mast. Odysseus cut off a length of it, sharpened one end, hardened the point in the fire, and hid it under the dung.

That evening the Cyclops came home and ate two more men, and Odysseus walked right up to him holding a bowl of the dark wine.

Here, Cyclops, he said. Have a drink, after your dinner of men.

The Cyclops drank it and loved it. He asked for another, and then a third. And then, very kindly, he asked: what's your name, stranger? I'd like to give you a present.

My name, said Odysseus, is Nobody.

Then here's your present, Nobody, the Cyclops said. I'll eat you last.

And he fell over backwards, drunk, and started snoring.

They pushed the stake into the coals until it glowed. Four men lifted it with Odysseus and drove it into the eye, and Odysseus leaned his weight on it and twisted, the way a shipwright works a drill.

The Cyclops screamed so loudly the whole mountain rang. His neighbours came running and shouted through the stone: Polyphemus, what's wrong? Is somebody killing you?

And from inside, he roared back: Nobody is killing me!

Well, they said, if nobody's hurting you, it must be a sickness sent by the gods. You'd better pray to your father. And they went home.

In the morning the blind giant rolled the stone aside and sat in the doorway with his hands out, feeling every sheep that went past.

Odysseus had tied his men under the bellies of the rams, three rams to each man. For himself he took the biggest ram of the flock and hung underneath it, gripping the wool, and the giant's hands passed right over his back.

They ran for the ships, and they got away.

*[BEAT: the turn — this is the moment that curses the whole voyage]*

And this is the moment Odysseus threw everything away.

As the ship pulled out, he stood up and shouted back at the island. Cyclops! If anyone asks who blinded you, tell them it wasn't Nobody. It was Odysseus, sacker of cities, son of Laertes, from Ithaca!

His men begged him to shut up. The giant tore off the top of a hill and threw it at the sound of his voice, and the wave nearly carried the ship back onto the beach.

But Odysseus wanted the credit. He'd won with a fake name, and he couldn't stand nobody knowing his real one.

Now the Cyclops knew exactly who to curse. He lifted his hands to the sea and prayed to his father: Poseidon, if I'm really your son, never let Odysseus reach home. Or if he has to get there, let him get there late, and alone, on somebody else's ship, and let him find trouble in his house.

And Poseidon heard him.

## 4 · Aeolus and the Bag of Winds

For a while it looked like their luck had turned. They reached a floating island with walls of bronze, the home of Aeolus, keeper of the winds.

Aeolus liked Odysseus. He kept him for a month, asking for story after story about Troy, and when it was time to leave he gave him a leather bag tied shut with a silver cord. Inside it he'd trapped every wind that could blow them off course. He left only the west wind free, to carry them straight home.

They sailed for nine days and nine nights, and Odysseus held the steering oar himself the whole time, because he didn't trust anyone else with it.

On the tenth day they saw Ithaca. They were close enough to see the smoke of the fires and people moving on the shore.

And Odysseus, after nine days without sleep, closed his eyes.

*[BEAT: so close — hold on the island in the distance]*

His men looked at the bag and started whispering. What did the king get from Aeolus that he's hiding from us? Gold, probably. Silver. We fought the same war he did, and we're going home empty-handed.

So they untied the silver cord.

Every wind in the world came out at once. The storm picked up the ships and blew them all the way back across the sea, right back to the island of Aeolus.

Odysseus went and begged him for help a second time. Aeolus took one look at him and told him to get out. A man the gods hate that much, he said, I won't lift a finger for.

They'd been close enough to see the smoke from their own homes, and now they were further away than when they'd started.

## 5 · The Laestrygonians

There was no wind now, so they rowed. Six days of it, until they reached a harbour with high cliffs on both sides and a narrow mouth, the water inside as calm as a pond.

Eleven ships went in. Odysseus, careful for once, tied his own ship to a rock outside the entrance.

The land belonged to the Laestrygonians, who were giants as well, though these ones didn't bother with a cave.

They came to the cliffs by the thousands and threw boulders down, and the ships broke apart like eggshells. Then they speared the men in the water like fish and carried them home for dinner.

Odysseus cut his mooring rope with his sword and screamed at his crew to row. They got away, but they were the only ship that did.

Of the twelve ships, one was left, with maybe forty men on board.

## 6 · Circe

The next island was low and wooded, with a thread of smoke rising from the middle of the forest. Nobody wanted to go and look, and you can't blame them. The last two times they'd gone to look, giants had eaten them.

So they drew lots, and half the crew went, led by a man named Eurylochus.

Deep in the forest they found a house of polished stone. Lions and wolves prowled around it, but they didn't attack. They came up wagging their tails like dogs. Inside, a woman was singing at a loom.

Her name was Circe. She invited them in and gave them cheese and honey and wine. Only Eurylochus stayed outside, because something about it felt wrong to him.

The wine was drugged. Once they'd drunk it, Circe touched each of them with a wand and they turned into pigs, with the heads and the bristles and the grunting. Their minds stayed human, which is the cruel part. She drove them into a sty and threw them acorns.

Eurylochus ran all the way back to the ship. Odysseus picked up his sword and went alone.

On the path a young man stopped him. It was Hermes, messenger of the gods, sent to help. He gave Odysseus a little plant with a black root and a white flower, called moly. Eat this, he said, and her drug won't touch you. And when she raises that wand, draw your sword as if you're going to kill her.

Odysseus drank the wine. Circe touched him with the wand and said: now go and lie down with your friends in the sty.

Nothing happened.

He drew his sword, and Circe dropped to her knees, because she understood who he was. You must be Odysseus, she said. Hermes always told me you'd come.

She turned his men back, younger and taller than before, and then she asked them all to stay.

They stayed a whole year.

*[BEAT: the second trap — comfort, like the lotus]*

In the end it was his own men who had to remind him. Odysseus, they said, remember home.

When he asked Circe to let him go, she said he was free to leave, but he couldn't go home yet. First he had to go somewhere no living man had ever been: the house of the dead.

## 7 · The Underworld

They sailed to the end of the world, where the sun never shines, to the edge of the river Ocean.

Odysseus dug a pit in the ground with his sword and poured offerings into it: milk and honey, then wine, then water, then barley. Then he cut the throats of a ram and a black ewe and let their blood run into the pit.

And the dead came up out of the dark in their thousands. There were brides and young men, old men who'd suffered a lot, soldiers still in their bloody armour, all of them making a terrible noise and pushing toward the blood. The dead are empty in Homer. Only blood lets them remember who they were, and speak.

Odysseus held them back with his sword, because he was waiting for one particular ghost.

The first to reach him was Elpenor, the youngest of his crew. On their last night at Circe's he'd gone to sleep drunk on the roof, and in the morning he forgot where he was, stepped off the edge, and broke his neck. They'd sailed without even noticing he was gone. He begged Odysseus to go back and bury him.

Then came someone Odysseus didn't expect at all. His mother. She'd been alive when he left for Troy.

He kept her back with the sword too. He let her stand there and wouldn't let her drink, because the one he'd come for hadn't arrived yet. I find that one of the hardest moments in the whole poem.

At last Tiresias came, the blind prophet, carrying a golden staff. He drank the blood, and he told Odysseus the truth.

Poseidon will never forgive you for his son, he said. But you can still get home, you and your men, if you manage one thing. On the island of the Sun you'll find the Sun god's cattle. Leave them alone, and you'll all get home. Touch them, and your ship will be destroyed and your men will die, and you'll come home late, alone, on a stranger's ship, and find men in your house eating your bread and courting your wife.

*[BEAT: the prophecy = the map of the rest of the video. Show it as a frieze panel and return to it]*

Only then did he let his mother drink.

She knew him straight away. She told him she hadn't died of illness or old age. She'd died of missing him, waiting for him to come home.

Three times he reached out to hold her, and three times she slipped through his arms like a shadow, or a dream.

After her came the heroes. Agamemnon, the king who'd led the whole war, murdered in his own house by his own wife on the day he came home. And then Achilles, the greatest of all the Greeks.

Odysseus tried to comfort him. Nobody was ever as lucky as you, he said. When you were alive we honoured you like a god, and down here you're a king among the dead.

Achilles told him not to try to make death sound good. He said he'd rather be the poorest hired hand on earth, working a field for a man with nothing, than be king over all of the dead.

Odysseus climbed back up to the light with that answer in his ears.

## 8 · The Sirens

Circe had warned him about what came next.

First, the Sirens. Two creatures who sat in a meadow beside the sea and sang. Every sailor who heard them steered straight toward them, and the meadow around them was piled with bones, the skin still drying on them.

The crew plugged their ears with wax. Odysseus, of course, wanted to hear it. So he had them tie him to the mast, hand and foot, and he told them: if I beg you to untie me, tie me tighter.

The wind dropped and the sea went flat, and then they heard the song.

What they sang about was him. Come here, famous Odysseus, glory of the Greeks. We know everything you suffered at Troy, and everything that happens anywhere on earth. Stop and listen, and you'll leave knowing more than you came with.

They'd picked the bait well. They offered him his own fame and the chance to know everything, and those were the two things he wanted most.

He begged them to untie him. He shouted and pulled at the ropes until they cut into his skin. Two of his men got up and tied him tighter.

Slowly the song faded behind them. They took the wax out of their ears and kept rowing, toward the narrowest place in the sea.

## 9 · Scylla and Charybdis

Odysseus had to sail through a strait that no ship had ever crossed whole.

On one side lived Charybdis, a whirlpool that swallowed the sea three times a day, and with it every ship that came too close.

On the other side, high on a cliff, lived Scylla, a monster with six long necks and six heads, each with three rows of teeth.

The witch Circe had warned him: you cannot fight her, and you cannot outrun them both. Choose one.

Charybdis would take the whole ship. Scylla would take six men.

So Odysseus made his choice, and he told his crew nothing.

He put on his armour, took two spears, and stood at the bow, as if he could protect them.

They steered toward the cliff, staring in terror at the whirlpool.

Then the six heads came down.

In a single moment, six of his best men were lifted into the air, screaming his name, reaching out to him.

And he could not save a single one.

The ship shot out of the strait, and the crew rowed on in silence.

Of everything he suffered on the long road home, it was the most pitiful thing he ever saw.

## 10 · The Cattle of the Sun

Ahead lay an island, green and quiet, and from the water they could hear cattle lowing.

Odysseus remembered the prophecy and told them they weren't landing. They'd sail straight past.

But his men were worn out. Eurylochus stood up and said what all of them were thinking. You're made of iron, Odysseus, and we aren't. Let us sleep one night on dry land.

Odysseus gave in, but he made every one of them swear an oath that nobody would touch the cattle.

That night a storm came, and after the storm the wind settled in the south, the one direction that couldn't carry them home. It blew from there for a month.

They ate everything on the ship. They fished, and they hunted birds, and they starved anyway.

One day, while Odysseus was off alone praying, Eurylochus made a speech. Every kind of death is terrible, he said, but starving is the worst. Let's kill the best of the cattle and offer some to the gods. And if the Sun is angry, I'd rather drown in one gulp than waste away on this island.

When Odysseus came back, he could smell the roasting meat from a long way off.

*[BEAT: the prophecy panel returns — the cattle panel lights up]*

What happened next is the strangest scene in the poem. The hides of the dead cattle crawled across the ground, and the meat on the spits mooed.

The Sun went to Zeus and told him to punish them. If you don't, he said, I'll go down into the house of the dead and shine there instead.

When the wind finally changed, they sailed. As soon as the island dropped behind them, Zeus threw his thunderbolt.

The mast came down and crushed the helmsman's skull. The ship filled with smoke and the stink of sulphur, and the men were thrown into the water. For a while the waves carried them around the wreck like seabirds, and then they went under.

All of them.

Odysseus lashed the broken mast to the keel and held on, and all night the current dragged him back the way he'd come. Back to the strait, and back to Charybdis.

When the whirlpool sucked his raft down, he jumped and grabbed a fig tree growing out of the cliff, and he hung there like a bat all day, waiting for the sea to spit his wood back up.

Eventually it did. He dropped down onto it and paddled away with his hands.

He drifted for nine days.

## 11 · Calypso

On the tenth night the sea threw him up on the island of Calypso.

She was a nymph, a goddess, beautiful, and she lived alone in a cave hung with vines and surrounded by springs. She saved his life, and fed him, and fell in love with him. And she wouldn't let him leave.

He was there for seven years.

She offered him everything. Stay with me, she said, and I'll make you immortal. You'll never get old, and you'll never die.

And every day Odysseus went down to the beach, sat on the rocks, looked out at the sea, and cried. All he wanted was to see the smoke rising from Ithaca one more time, and then he was happy to die.

He turned down living forever so he could go back to a small rocky island and a wife who was getting older without him. I've always liked him most here.

In the end the gods took pity on him. Poseidon was away at a feast at the far end of the world, and while he was gone, Athena, goddess of wisdom, who'd always had a soft spot for Odysseus because he was so clever, went to Zeus. Zeus sent Hermes to Calypso with an order to let him go.

Calypso was furious. You gods can't stand it, she said, when a goddess loves a mortal man. But she did as she was told.

She gave him tools, and he cut down trees and built a raft with his own hands. On the eighteenth day at sea he saw land.

And Poseidon, coming home from his feast, saw him.

He gathered up every cloud in the sky and stirred the sea with his trident, and the raft came apart. For two days and two nights Odysseus swam. On the third day, more dead than alive, he dragged himself up the mouth of a river onto a beach, buried himself in a pile of dry leaves, and slept.

## 12 · The Phaeacians

He woke up to the sound of girls playing ball. A princess named Nausicaa had come down to the river with her maids to wash clothes.

Then a naked man, crusted with salt, stepped out of the bushes holding a leafy branch in front of himself. The maids ran. The princess stood her ground.

She gave him clothes and brought him to her father, king of the Phaeacians, who were the finest sailors in the world.

At the feast that night a blind singer picked up his lyre and sang about the Trojan War. He sang about the wooden horse, and about the clever man who'd thought of it.

The stranger at the table pulled his cloak over his face and wept.

The king asked him, gently, who he was.

*[BEAT: frame — he tells the story we just saw; quick black-figure recap montage of chapters 2–11]*

For the first time since the Cyclops, he said his name out loud. I am Odysseus. Then he told them everything that had happened since Troy, ending with the cattle of the Sun, and the name of every man he'd lost along the way.

The Phaeacians sat listening, in silence, until midnight.

The next evening they put him on one of their ships, loaded with gifts, and he fell into a sleep so deep it was almost like death, while the ship skimmed over the water faster than a hawk.

When he woke up, he was lying on a beach he didn't recognise.

It was Ithaca. He'd come home late, and alone, on somebody else's ship, just as the Cyclops had prayed.

## 13 · Ithaca

Athena appeared to him and told him what was waiting at his house.

He'd been gone twenty years, ten at the war and ten at sea, and everyone believed he was dead. So more than a hundred young men from the islands had moved into his palace to court his wife, Penelope.

They ate his cattle and drank his wine, they slept with his servant girls, and every single day they demanded that she pick one of them to marry.

For years she'd tricked them. She told them she'd choose a husband once she finished weaving a burial shroud for Odysseus's old father. She worked on it every day, and every night, in secret, she unpicked what she'd woven. It worked for three years, until one of her maids gave her away.

His son, Telemachus, had been a baby when Odysseus sailed for Troy. Now he was a grown man, and the suitors were planning to murder him.

*[BEAT: the stakes — show the full palace frieze with the suitors]*

Athena touched Odysseus with her wand. His skin wrinkled, his hair fell out, and his eyes went cloudy, and she dressed him in filthy rags so that nobody would know him.

He walked into his own town as a beggar.

At the gate of the palace, lying on a heap of dung, was an old dog, crawling with fleas and too weak to stand. It was Argos. Odysseus had raised him from a puppy, just before he left for Troy.

After twenty years, not one person had recognised Odysseus. The dog knew him at once.

He flattened his ears and wagged his tail, but he couldn't get up to go to him.

And Odysseus couldn't go to the dog without giving himself away. So he walked past, and wiped away a tear before anyone could see it.

And Argos, having seen his master again after twenty years, died.

Inside, the suitors made fun of the beggar. One threw a stool at him, another a footstool, another a cow's hoof. Odysseus took all of it without a word, and he watched them.

That night Penelope sat down to talk with the stranger, with no idea who he was, and told him what she'd decided. Tomorrow, she said, I'll bring out my husband's bow. Whoever can string it and shoot an arrow through twelve axe heads in a row, the way Odysseus used to, I'll marry him.

The next day the suitors tried, one after another. They warmed the bow by the fire and rubbed it with fat to soften it, and not one of them could even bend it.

Then the beggar asked for a turn.

They laughed at him, and they shouted. But Telemachus handed him the bow.

Odysseus turned it over in his hands, the way a musician picks up a lyre he knows. Then, without even getting up from his seat, he strung it, easily. He plucked the string, and it sang like a swallow.

Out of a clear sky, Zeus thundered.

He fitted an arrow, and shot it through all twelve axes without touching one.

*[BEAT: the peak — hold, silence, then the turn]*

Then he tore off his rags, leapt onto the threshold, and poured the arrows out at his feet.

The first one went into the throat of Antinous, the leader of the suitors, just as he was lifting a cup of wine to his mouth.

You dogs, said Odysseus. You thought I'd never come home.

The doors were locked, and the spears had been taken down off the walls the night before. Odysseus, his son, and two servants who'd stayed loyal fought more than a hundred men in that hall, and they kept fighting until the floor ran with blood and nobody else was left standing.

## 14 · Penelope's Bed

The old nurse ran upstairs to wake Penelope. He's here, she told her. Odysseus is home, and he's killed them all.

Penelope came down and sat across the fire from him, and looked at him for a long time, and said nothing.

Telemachus was furious with her. Mother, he said, how can you be so cold?

But she'd waited twenty years, and in that time gods had walked through her house in disguise. She wasn't going to throw her arms around a stranger because he said he was her husband.

So she tested him. She turned to the nurse and said: move our bed out of the bedroom so he can sleep on it.

And Odysseus, the man who'd tricked a giant and a witch and a hundred suitors, finally lost his temper. Who moved my bed? Nobody can move that bed. I built it myself, around the trunk of an olive tree that was growing right there in the house. The tree is one of its legs. To move it, you'd have to cut it down.

Only the two of them had ever known that.

Penelope ran to him then, and held him, and cried.

*[BEAT: closing image — the olive bed on the vase, the meander closes the frieze]*

He'd set out with twelve ships and six hundred men, and he'd lost every one of them along the way, some to the sea and the monsters, some to the gods, and more than a few to his own pride.

But he was home. And that night, Athena held back the dawn, so the night would last a little longer.
