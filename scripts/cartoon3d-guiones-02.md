# cartoon3d — lote 2 de guiones (2026-10-23): sin fechas, en presente, escenas cotidianas con truco oculto

## 1. The close door button
You're late, the elevator doors are open, and someone's running toward you down the hallway. So you start smashing the close door button. Nothing. You hit it again, harder, like it owes you money. The guy squeezes in, smiles, and says, thanks for holding it. Now you're standing there, staring at a button that just betrayed you. So, let's open the panel. In a lot of elevators, especially in the US, that button isn't even connected to anything. You see, the law says the doors have to stay open long enough for someone in a wheelchair or on crutches to get in. So the doors close on a timer, no matter what you press. But people hate standing there doing nothing, so the button stays. It's not for the doors. It's for you. Every time you jab it, the doors close at the exact same speed they always would have. You just feel like you did it.

## 2. The crosswalk button
You walk up to a crosswalk, press the button, and wait. Nothing. So you press it again. And again. The guy next to you, without even looking up from his phone, says, that thing's fake, man. You laugh. Then the light turns green, at the exact moment it was always going to. You see, years ago, those buttons actually changed the lights. But then cities hooked their traffic lights up to computers that run every intersection on a schedule. In New York, most of the buttons stopped doing anything. Thousands of them. But ripping them all out costs millions, so they just left them there. So now you've got a city full of buttons that people press every single day, that don't do anything at all, except make you feel a little less like you're just standing there.

## 3. The minibar
You check into a fancy hotel, open the little fridge, and pick up a chocolate bar just to read the price. Twelve dollars? No way. You put it right back and close the door. The next morning, at checkout, the guy at the desk smiles and says, and one chocolate bar from the minibar. But you didn't eat it. You see, in some hotels, every item in that fridge is sitting on a sensor. The second you lift something, it thinks you took it, and it bills your room automatically. Some even charge you if you just move it out of its spot and don't put it back fast enough. So you didn't buy a twelve dollar chocolate bar. You just held one. And in some hotels, holding it costs exactly the same.

## 4. The hole in the plane window
You're on a plane, staring out the window, and you notice a tiny hole drilled right through it. Like someone poked it with a needle. So you start wondering, is there supposed to be a hole in the thing keeping you alive at thirty thousand feet? The lady next to you is fast asleep, and you're the only one panicking. Well, that window is actually three windows stacked together. The one you can touch is just a plastic cover. Behind it is a middle pane with the hole, and then the outer pane, the one holding the whole sky back. You see, the hole lets air pass between the cabin and the gap, so all the pressure pushes on the outer pane alone. And if that one ever cracks, the middle pane is right behind it, ready to take over. So the hole isn't a mistake. It's the reason there's a backup.

## 5. The menu with no dollar signs
You sit down at a fancy restaurant, open the menu, and something feels off. The steak just says twenty eight. Not twenty eight dollars. Just a number, floating there. So you order the steak, a drink, maybe dessert, and somehow the bill is way higher than you planned. You see, researchers gave diners three kinds of menus. One with dollar signs, one with prices written out in words, and one with just the numbers. The people with the plain numbers spent the most. Because every time you see a dollar sign, your brain remembers it's real money leaving your wallet. It's the pain of paying. Take the sign away, and twenty eight is just a number. So the menu isn't missing something. It's hiding it on purpose.

## 6. The phone at 1 percent
It's late, it's raining, and your phone is at 1 percent. You open the ride app, and the price is way higher than usual. Normally you'd wait it out. But the screen is about to die, so you smash accept before it goes black. Here's the weird part. The ride company actually noticed this. You see, the app can see your battery level, and their own economist said that people with a dying phone are way more likely to accept a higher price. Because when your battery is about to run out, waiting for the price to drop isn't an option anymore. The company said it doesn't use that to raise prices. But it knows. So the next time your phone is at 1 percent and the price looks high, it's not just the rain. It's you, deciding faster than you normally would.
