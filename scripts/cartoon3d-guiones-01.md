## 1. The rat tails (Hanoi, 1902)
The French paid people to kill rats, and ended up with more rats than ever. It's 1902, and Hanoi just got brand new sewers, which turn out to be a five-star hotel for rats. So the city hires hunters to go down there and pays them for every rat they kill. Thousands a day. Still not enough. So they open it up to everyone. One cent per rat, and you don't even have to bring the whole thing. Just the tail. People are lining up at the office with buckets of tails. Then, someone notices something weird. Rats running around the streets with no tails. Healthy, happy, totally alive rats. You see, if you kill a rat, you get paid once. But if you just cut off its tail and let it go, it goes home and makes more rats. With more tails. So some people stopped hunting rats and started farming them. The city was literally paying them to breed the problem.

## 2. The town that wasn't there (Agloe, New York)
Two mapmakers invented a fake town, and then it showed up. Back in the 1930s, anyone could copy your map and sell it as their own. So two guys hid a trap in theirs. A tiny town called Agloe, on an empty dirt road in New York. No houses, no people, just a dot made from their initials. If Agloe ever popped up on someone else's map, they'd know it was stolen. Years go by, and there it is. Agloe, on a rival company's map. Gotcha. Except the other company says, we didn't copy anything. We went there. There's a store. You see, someone driving down that road saw Agloe on a map, figured it was a real town, and opened a little shop called the Agloe General Store. So the fake town on the map made a real place on the road. And suddenly, the trap didn't prove anything at all.

## 3. Why plane windows are round (de Havilland Comet, 1954)
Airplane windows are round because the first jet airliner kept falling out of the sky. In 1952, Britain launched the Comet, the first passenger jet ever. It flew higher and faster than anything, with big, beautiful square windows. Then, in 1954, one broke apart over the sea. A few months later, another one. Same plane, clear skies, no explanation. So engineers took a whole Comet, sank it in a giant tank of water, and pumped it up and down, over and over, like it was taking off and landing every day for years. Thousands of fake flights later, the cabin ripped open, right at the corner of a window. You see, every time a plane climbs, the air inside pushes on the walls, and all that stress flows around the windows. On a round window, it slides around the curve. On a square one, it piles up in the corners, and every flight cracks the metal just a tiny bit more. So every window you've ever looked out of on a plane is round, because square ones tear the plane open one flight at a time.

## 4. The dead man's briefcase (Operation Mincemeat, 1943)
The British fooled Hitler by letting a dead man lose his briefcase. In 1943, everyone knew the Allies were about to invade Sicily. It was the obvious move. Way too obvious. So British spies got a body, dressed him up as an officer named Major William Martin, and gave him a whole fake life. A photo of his girlfriend, her love letters, an angry note from his bank, even used theater tickets. And chained to his wrist, a briefcase of top secret letters saying the real attack would hit Greece and Sardinia. Then a submarine slipped him into the sea off Spain, right where German spies were watching. A fisherman found him, the letters got copied, and they went all the way to Hitler. And Hitler bought it. He sent troops away to defend places nobody was attacking. You see, secret letters alone look fake. But nobody fakes a guy's overdue bills. So one of the most successful lies of the whole war was a man who never existed.

## 5. The war Australia lost to birds (the Emu War, 1932)
In 1932, Australia declared war on birds, and lost. After World War One, the government gave soldiers farmland out west to grow wheat. Then twenty thousand emus showed up and started eating all of it. So the army sends in a major, two soldiers, and two machine guns. Easy, right? They set up an ambush at a dam, and a thousand emus walk right in. They open fire, and the birds split into tiny groups and sprint off in every direction at fifty kilometers an hour. Then the gun jams. So they bolt a machine gun onto a truck and chase them. The truck gets stuck in the sand, and the emus just jog away. After almost ten thousand bullets, the emus were basically fine. You see, emus run as fast as a car, scatter instead of bunching up, and can keep going after getting hit. So the major went home and said that if he had soldiers who could take bullets like those birds, he could beat any army in the world.

## 6. The king who broke Cairo (Mansa Musa, 1324)
A king once got so rich that he broke a whole city's economy, just by visiting. In 1324, Mansa Musa, the king of Mali, went on a pilgrimage to Mecca. He brought thousands of people and camels loaded with gold, and he gave it away everywhere he went. When he passed through Cairo, he handed out so much gold that suddenly everyone in the city had some. Sounds amazing. Until you go to buy bread. You see, gold is only valuable because it's rare. When everyone has it, it stops being special, so shopkeepers start asking for more of it for the same loaf. The price of gold in Cairo crashed, and it stayed low for years. Some stories even say he tried to fix it on the way home by borrowing gold back from the moneylenders. So the richest man in history didn't just spend his money. He made everyone else's worth less.
